The [Humanitarian OpenStreetMap Team (HOT)](https://www.hotosm.org/), in collaboration with the [Universidad Mayor de San Andrés (UMSA)](https://www.umsa.bo/) in Bolivia and the [Universidad Autónoma Latinoamericana (UNAULA)](https://www.unaula.edu.co/) in Colombia, is implementing the project [Participatory Mapping for a Sustainable Amazon](https://www.hotosm.org/projects/mapeos-participativos-para-una-amazonia-sostenible/). This initiative is funded by the [Inter-American Institute for Global Change Research (IAI)](https://www.iai.int/es) through its Seed Grant, *Tropical Forests in the Americas: Transdisciplinary Approaches to Environmental Transformations*, SG-TF-2024.

The project aims to build maps that make visible socio-environmental conflicts, local protection initiatives, and opportunities for public policy engagement. It does so through the use of open-source technologies and the exchange of knowledge between academic and community actors. In this context, fieldwork for the participatory mapping subprojects in Bolivia and Colombia began in May.

These field activities are part of training cycles designed for each country. They aim to strengthen the capacity of local communities in the use of open geospatial tools. At the same time, and under a transdisciplinary approach, the project promotes knowledge exchange with these communities to identify critical conservation areas, socio-environmental conflicts—such as deforestation, mining, illicit crops, or the impacts of climate change—as well as the protection actions that communities themselves are undertaking.

## Bolivia

In Bolivia, the UMSA team chose to work with the community of Sapecho, located in the municipality of Palos Blancos, Sud Yungas province, in the department of La Paz. The decision was based on its proximity to the university’s experimental station, which facilitates technical and logistical support. This region is known for its natural diversity, including tropical forests and mountain ranges, as well as a rich cultural heritage shaped by Indigenous and intercultural communities.

The community of Sapecho is predominantly composed of people from the Tacana Indigenous group. For this reason, the [Indigenous Council of the Tacana People (CIPTA)](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consejo_Ind%C3%ADgena_del_Pueblo_Tacana) designated the community members who would participate in the workshop. As part of the project's interdisciplinary and collaborative approach, park rangers and firefighters from La Paz—who regularly respond to forest fires in the area—were also invited, recognizing the value of coordinated efforts in territorial management.

The workshop, designed and facilitated by HOT and UMSA, was conducted using a participatory, interdisciplinary, and hands-on approach. It combined theoretical sessions, field activities, and community dialogue. The methodology aimed to integrate technological knowledge—such as the use of drones via [Drone Tasking Manager](https://dronetm.org/), OpenStreetMap, and [ChatMap](https://www.hotosm.org/tech-suite/chatmap/)—with local knowledge, to empower participants and generate useful data for fire prevention and integrated territorial management.

Engineer Patricia Llanos, the project lead in Bolivia who also directed the field activities in Sapecho, emphasized that this initial workshop marked the beginning of a deep and transformative process. She noted that the experience helped capture the relationship communities have with their environment and laid the foundation for long-term collaboration:

> *Through the experiences shared by local communities, regional residents, and students from the Sapecho experimental station, we captured the essence of their connection to the land. We want to reflect these experiences in maps that portray a rich and diverse reality. Transforming this data into meaningful information for risk prevention, spatial planning, and environmental conservation is a crucial challenge that we aim to support.*

Patricia also noted the strong commitment shown by participants and reiterated that a sustainable future for the Amazon must be built on local knowledge and inter-institutional collaboration.

Emilio Mariscal, HOT’s Software Engineering Manager, highlighted that the activity in Bolivia was more than just a test of tools—it was a true exchange of knowledge. He stressed that impactful technology development cannot happen in isolation or solely from behind a screen. In his words:

> *Learning about geospatial information systems is not usually part of people’s lives—especially for those whose priority is to protect their homes, forests, and lives. I believe we need to build much more user-friendly solutions.*

During the Sapecho workshop, Emilio introduced ChatMap, a mapping tool that works via messaging platforms like WhatsApp, Telegram, or Signal. Shortly afterward, while watching the [Participatory Mapping for a Sustainable Amazon webinar](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hN7RbBTKFhM) from Argentina, he witnessed an unexpected moment: a young Indigenous Bolivian participant shared how he had learned to use ChatMap during the workshop and his plans to map illegal mining in his community.

> *This kind of use case—what I call ‘organic’ because no one pushed or imposed the software, someone just found it useful and adopted it—is incredibly compelling.*

## Colombia

In Colombia, the UNAULA team, in coordination with HOT staff, traveled to the territory of Santa Rosa, home of the Wairari Atún Sacha Cabildo, located in the municipality of Santa Rosa, Cauca Department. This community belongs to the Inga people, and their cabildo house—an important center of political and cultural organization—is located in the village of La Tarabita. The Wairari Atún Sacha Cabildo was formed as an organizational response to the region's challenges, where previously there was no Indigenous representation in the municipal seat.

La Tarabita is known for its remarkable ecological and cultural diversity. It encompasses Andean mountain ecosystems, remnants of humid forest, and critical watershed areas. This territory is marked by historic and ongoing tensions between extractive economies, geopolitical interests, Indigenous resistance, and land-based ways of life centered on care and reciprocity.

During the intensive participatory mapping workshop, a wide range of community-centered technological tools were used to facilitate the collection, organization, and visualization of territorial information. These included drones for generating orthophotos, OpenStreetMap, Mapillary, OSMTracker, OsmAnd, Tasking Manager, ChatMap, and QGIS.

Leonardo Jiménez, Director of the POMOTE Study Center at UNAULA, shared that the workshop in Santa Rosa was much more than a technical exercise. From his perspective, it was a transformative experience for both the facilitation team and the community:

> *The first visit to the Wairari Atún Sacha Cabildo territory exceeded all expectations. There, the map ceased to be just a measuring tool and became a medium for dialogue and identity affirmation. Listening to the voices of the cabildo authorities, walking the trails that hold the memory of ancestors, and feeling the pulse of the land in each shared reference point helped us understand that mapping is not a neutral technique, but a form of political and emotional engagement with the territory.*

One of the most valuable lessons, Leonardo noted, was witnessing how mapping tools—when adopted by the community—become instruments of power to defend life, memory, and autonomy. The enthusiasm with which young people from the cabildo explored GPS use, base maps, and mobile applications was not a response to trends but a conscious step toward territorial strengthening.

> *A mapping project in the Amazon cannot be separated from the historic struggles for Indigenous self-determination, nor from the tensions that impact their territories. What was at stake during this visit was more than just a map—it was the opportunity to build rooted, dignified, and reciprocal knowledge.*

For him, the experience marked the start of a deep and necessary conversation on territorial sustainability—built not on data extraction, but on mutual knowledge sharing.

Juan Melo, part of the HOT team, recalled how during the first few days of the workshop, automated drone flights were carried out near the cabildo. Participants, initially cautious, gradually became more curious and engaged with the new technologies.

> *On the fourth day, we held a “minca de la palabra” (a communal sharing session). We showed them what we had done, shared images and videos of their territory seen from the sky, and discussed the importance of protecting this space that means so much to everyone. It was a deeply emotional and powerful moment. Some people, seeing their land from the air for the first time, were visibly moved.*

By the fifth day, the group had changed. Familiarity with the tools and the process was evident: participants were confidently piloting drones, understanding the logic behind mapping, and clearly aware of why they were doing it. The learning curve had been overcome with commitment and dedication.

The technical results of the exercise came quickly: an orthophoto of the territory was generated and published on OpenAerialMap, and images taken on the ground were uploaded to Mapillary. However, for Juan, the most important outcome was not the products, but the process:

> *What remains is a living experience—an exercise that strengthened the connection between technology and territory, between technical knowledge and ancestral wisdom.*

Reflecting on the work in Bolivia and Colombia, Céline Jacquin, HOT's Senior Manager for Latin America, believes that this project marks a milestone by combining participatory mapping with a transdisciplinary approach. Beyond its methodological value, she emphasizes its practical impact and long-term vision for the Amazonian territories:

> *Its greatest strength lies in its replicability. By integrating open technologies—such as DroneTaskingManager, ChatMap, or OpenStreetMap—with the knowledge and practices of Indigenous communities, we generate tangible tools to strengthen territorial autonomy, prevent socio-environmental conflicts, influence political processes, and access support for community-driven development models.*

For Céline, the field results speak for themselves. What was witnessed in Sapecho and Santa Rosa shows that when communities take ownership of these tools, they not only protect their territories more effectively, but also document threats—such as deforestation or illegal mining—with greater speed, precision, and legitimacy.

> *This kind of mapping goes beyond the technical—it becomes a form of community monitoring and active territorial defense.*

In her view, supporting initiatives like this means committing to sustainable, replicable solutions that are deeply rooted in local realities. Each map becomes a tool for action; each data point, a lever for advocating more just policies; and each trained community, a stronger voice in the face of climate change. The Amazon needs concrete action. Now is the time to promote proposals that blend technological innovation, community participation, and a deep commitment to the territory.
