## Humanitarian GIS Reflections in Mid-2025: A Collaborative Approach to Geospatial Data

The first Humanitarian Networks & Partnerships Weeks (HNPW) I attended in person was in May 2022, when we were coming out of the pandemic. At the time, I felt an energy in the room - a sense that genuine collaboration between organizations was finally within reach after so long living in digital spaces. As excited as we all were to be back in person in our offices and at conferences, there was a clear interest in continuing a remote but connected way of working. 

Three years later, in 2025, that excitement to work together is being quickly prompted into reality as we find ourselves in a very different sector with massive funding cuts. Attending this year’s HNPW in March, these were some of the themes that came up repeatedly throughout the conference:

1. **Stopping duplication** through increased coordination
2. Improved working with **local actors**
3. **Co-funding** as the future model

The humanitarian data sector faces particular challenges, with [USAID and donor cuts](https://www.acaps.org/en/us-funding-freeze) devastating response capabilities. As noted in [Humanitarian data drought: The deeper damage wrought by US aid cuts](https://www.thenewhumanitarian.org/analysis/2025/03/25/humanitarian-data-drought-deeper-damage-wrought-us-aid-cuts), data systems are often first to be cut as "non-essential."

Yet spatial data offers a path forward. **Maps have always brought people together** - whether showing infrastructure, climate factors, market prices, or security trends. OpenStreetMap (OSM) stands out as a premier example of a **shared resource**, with its open data model that anyone can use. [Research shows](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10708-021-10547-7) why OSM has become the go-to global spatial data source.

OSM's value is demonstrated by its popularity on the Humanitarian Data Exchange (HDX), where [OpenStreetMap exports by HOT](https://data.humdata.org/organization/stats/hot) received nearly 5,000 visits in the last 30 days. The model of **locally led systems with global support** - seen in growing OSM communities in [Sudan](https://www.hotosm.org/projects/geospatial-data-for-humanitarian-response-in-sudan-conflict/) and [Lebanon](https://hsph.harvard.edu/atrocity-prevention-lab/news/mapping-hope-rebuilding-lebanon-after-conflict/) - offers a blueprint for sustainable humanitarian mapping.

## Call to Action: Three Steps for Collaborative GIS

1. **Identify core spatial data layers** needed across humanitarian responses, starting with HDX's [data grids](https://data.humdata.org/dashboards/overview-of-data-grids) and top OSM datasets (roads, buildings, waterways, etc.)
2. **Share data openly** and communicate needs through collaborative networks
3. **Find partners** across organizations to maximize impact

Examples from HOT's [Conflict and Displacement Program](https://www.hotosm.org/programs/conflict-displacement) include:
- Updated OSM building footprints in high-displacement areas of [Somalia](https://www.hotosm.org/projects/osm-buildings-for-representation-of-idps-in-somalia/)
- Critical mapping in [Gaza](https://www.hotosm.org/updates/gaza-osm-buildings-update-2024/)
- Heritage site documentation in [Lebanon and Sudan](https://www.flickr.com/photos/200430814@N03/54580525638/)
- [Logistics mapping](https://www.flickr.com/photos/200430814@N03/54580525648/) and bilingual [road naming](https://www.flickr.com/photos/200430814@N03/54580633330/) in Sudan
